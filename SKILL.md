# Food Watercolor Study

## Overview

Create a poetic **Naive Watercolor Diary / 朴拙水彩手帐** page, not a photo filter, literal redraw, or polished commercial illustration. Preserve recognizable subjects while simplifying them into imperfect hand-painted signs. For food, retain the cooking state, main ingredients, and basic serving relationship. For a still-life test, retain each supplied object family and its defining motif.

## Workflow

1. Select a subject mode:
   - **Food-photo mode:** inspect the supplied image; identify the prepared dish, cooking state, hero ingredient, and 1–2 essential pairings. Keep those relationships. Use one food hero plus a small edited breakdown.
   - **Element collage mode:** use this when the user gives a short list of food, drink, or household elements without a source photo. Turn every named element into a separate small vignette or one of its simple variations; do not force the list into one drink, plate, or hero dish.
   - **Still-life test mode:** inspect the supplied object list, photo, or reference; keep 3–6 named object families as separate symbolic diary studies. Preserve only the recognition-bearing motif of each family—glass outline and waterline, a lampshade pattern, paired birds, flame, foliage, or textile stripe. Do not require one hero and do not invent extra categories.
2. Extract a compact visual vocabulary:
   - In food-photo mode, keep one hero presentation of the prepared food.
   - Select 1–3 food-derived echoes: a cut face, seed, ice cube, leaf, sauce drop, rind, steam mark, utensil, or simplified ingredient. Keep only the most expressive supporting food states; do not inventory every component.
   - In food-photo mode, add 2–4 restrained associative marks derived from the food's form or meaning: a few stars or dots, one botanical sprig, one tiny diagram, or one translucent paper patch. Do not add arbitrary narrative objects.
   - In element collage mode, use 4–6 loose vignettes for a list of 3–5 elements. A named food may appear once as a small group and once as a single study; this is a permitted variation, not an extra ingredient. Do not add stars, dots, paper patches, or symbols unless the user names them or they are intrinsic to a supplied object.
   - In still-life mode, use 6–14 small faded-cobalt stars, asterisks, dots, or short dashes between object clusters as page rhythm. Vary their size, tilt, spacing, and line weight; never join them into a decorative connector.
   - Start by removing repeated food portions, not all supporting life. For a detailed prepared dish, prefer the hero plus two or three small supporting studies and a handful of quiet marks. Never show every ingredient, every serving, and every symbol at once.
3. Create a natural visual relationship using staggered placement, scale contrast, repeated colors or forms, partial overlap, and the direction of real food shapes. In element collage mode, create rhythm through alternating empty space, separate mini-scenes, and text blocks; do not connect clusters with a line. Use a literal sauce trail, stem, steam path, orbit, or dotted arc only when it genuinely belongs to the pictured food or object; never add one merely to manufacture motion.
4. Write the subject-linked English fortune using the rules below. Plan its fragments and reading order before rendering.
5. Generate the illustration text-free with the food photo as the content reference. Remove the original background completely.
6. Add the exact fortune in a second pass. Treat it as part of the collage rather than a caption.
7. Check the result against the quality bar. Regenerate if the food is unrecognizable, the page feels like a polished menu illustration, the symbols are arbitrary, the text is inaccurate, or the composition lacks a clear visual path.

## Composition System

Choose one visual path deliberately. Never default to one hero dish plus a rectangular caption area.

- **Orbit:** place a round food or drink off-center; let smaller ingredients, drops, seeds, or ice follow curved paths around it.
- **Falling sequence:** repeat 2–4 simplified food states vertically or diagonally, moving from whole to cut, raw to cooked, or solid to melted.
- **Scrapbook islands:** place the hero and selected details on irregular pale wash or torn-paper patches with uneven gaps between them.
- **Botanical drift:** let leaves, stems, real sauce, or steam create a natural diagonal relationship between separated food studies and text fragments; do not draw a connector just to link them.
- **Constellation:** scatter a hero, two food echoes, pigment dots, and small celestial marks across wide paper, using large scale contrast.
- **Quiet diagram:** combine one recognizably rendered food with simple childlike geometric studies of its shape, layers, seeds, or color.
- **Element sampler:** arrange four to six independent object-and-food vignettes across the paper. Let two short fortunes occupy the gaps. Use this only for a user-supplied prompt list without a source photo.

In food-photo mode, vary scale strongly: one hero may be three to six times larger than a supporting study. In still-life mode, distribute 4–6 floating object clusters at related scales, about 0.7–1.6 times one another, without a mandatory hero. In element collage mode, distribute 4–6 independent vignettes at related scales, with the largest no more than twice the smallest. Use asymmetry and generous unoccupied paper. Create continuity through visual rhythm—placement, repeated colors/forms, partial overlap, and the direction of leaves, slices, stems, or authentic drips—not a compulsory visible line. Rotate or stagger small elements when useful, but keep subjects upright enough to read. For a set, do not reuse the same visual path twice in a row.

## Art Direction

- Treat **Naive Watercolor Diary / 朴拙水彩手帐** as the default rendering character for every composition family.
- Use clean near-white fibrous paper with a cool-neutral ivory cast (`#F7F5EF` to `#FAF9F5`), never yellowed parchment or kraft-paper beige. Make the paper grain, very faint gray-beige fibers, deckled wash edges, and pigment granulation visible at normal size.
- Paint with low-fidelity translucent washes, soft bleeding edges, blooms, backruns, dry-brush gaps, and slight registration imperfections. Preserve charming awkwardness instead of polishing any contour.
- Prefer short uneven brush-hatch strokes, scribbled pigment fills, and lightly wobbly pencil-like details over smoothly blended rendered surfaces. Make patterned cloth, baskets, rind, and ice read through a few irregular marks rather than detailed texture.
- Keep the page chromatically clean: leave at least 55% as unpainted cool-white paper; do not glaze the whole image in ochre, tan, sepia, or cream. Use gray-blue or pale neutral washes for objects that are white, glass, ceramic, or background-adjacent.
- Reserve yellow, amber, and orange for named subject colors or true flame/light. If they are not explicit subjects, keep all warm pigments together below about 15% of painted area. Never tint the paper, shadows, plates, cups, or white objects yellow merely to make the page feel warm.
- Distort geometry gently. Make circles, ellipses, paired objects, rims, lampshades, and repeated motifs slightly uneven in width, tilt, or perspective. Never construct perfect concentric rings or mechanically identical repeats.
- Break outlines. Define only 40–70% of an object's edge; let pigment bleed, fade, double-register, or disappear into the paper elsewhere. Avoid continuous vector-like contours.
- Leave 10–25% of the paper showing through inside pale objects. Use at least three visibly different pigment densities within each major colored form, with pooled dark edges and lighter accidental gaps.
- Simplify internal information into a few expressive marks. Suggest seeds, veins, feathers, glass highlights, or painted motifs rather than rendering every detail precisely.
- Ground objects only with a pale irregular wash pool when necessary. Do not use smooth cast shadows, ambient-occlusion halos, studio lighting, glossy highlights, or modeled 3D volume.
- Keep the hero food recognizable through silhouette, cooking state, color relationships, and a few truthful internal details such as seeds, rind, char, layers, pulp, crumb, or ice. Render supporting studies more naively and diagrammatically.
- Mix observational food painting with flat childlike symbols. Use faint wash rectangles or irregular paper islands behind selected objects; do not frame every object.
- Work like a poetic visual diary: suggest associations through scale, repetition, and visual rhyme. An orange may echo a sun, ice may become a falling sequence, seeds may resemble stars, and steam may become a path—but the food must remain food.
- Limit each page to one dominant subject color, one cool accent, and clean paper neutral. Mix colors with pale gray, cool off-white, or a restrained complement so no large form looks digitally pure. Suggested palette: paper `#F7F5EF`, soft gray `#C8C9C4`, faded cobalt `#637EAF`, periwinkle `#9EACCE`, moss `#788B49`, pale lime `#B6C16B`, candle yellow `#D7B85A`, dusty red `#B85F51`, cocoa `#775B49`, charcoal `#34312E`.
- Prefer flat graphic space and very light grounding. Avoid glossy highlights, dense cast shadows, photographic depth, heavy black contours, neon color, smooth digital gradients, symmetrical grids, or polished commercial-menu rendering.

## Prompt Template

Adapt the bracketed fields; state the food identity before describing the style.

```text
Use case: style-transfer
Asset type: vertical poetic watercolor-diary illustration
Input images: Image 1 is the subject reference when supplied; any additional images are visual-language references only.
Primary request: remove any original background and reinterpret [FOOD / DRINK / STILL-LIFE OBJECT SET] as a poetic naive-watercolor diary collage.
Subject: in food-photo mode keep [HERO SUBJECT], [1–2 ESSENTIAL PAIRINGS], and broad relationships immediately recognizable. In element collage mode, keep every supplied item recognizable through a separate small vignette or a permitted simple variation. In still-life mode keep [3–6 NAMED OBJECT FAMILIES] recognizable through only their defining motifs. Simplify repeated portions or details without replacing named subjects with unrelated specimens.
Composition: in food-photo mode use [VISUAL PATH] with one focal subject, [1–3 SUBJECT-DERIVED ECHOES], and [2–4 RESTRAINED ASSOCIATIVE MARKS]. In element collage mode, use Element Sampler: arrange 4–6 independent vignettes at related scale, each with its own empty margin; use two short text blocks in the gaps and no unprompted stars, dots, or connector paths. In still-life mode arrange 4–6 separate floating studies at related scale with 6–14 irregular faded-cobalt rhythm marks between them. Never add a connector solely to create motion. Leave generous irregular paper space.
Style/medium: very naive low-fidelity watercolor on fibrous oatmeal paper; gently distorted geometry; 40–70% partial outlines; 10–25% paper gaps inside pale forms; at least three pigment densities per major color; blooms, backruns, pooled edges, dry-brush gaps, and slight double registration. Flat childlike silhouettes with almost no modeled volume.
Color palette: [DOMINANT SUBJECT COLOR], one faded cobalt or periwinkle accent, moss or pale lime where relevant, and clean cool-white paper neutrals. Keep white/glass/ceramic forms gray-blue or pale neutral, never globally yellowed. Use amber only when a named subject genuinely requires it; gray every color enough to avoid digital purity.
Text: none in the first pass; reserve calm spaces across the collage for the fortune.

```

## Subject Fortune

Pair every food-photo composition with one original English line that feels like a gentle fortune-cookie note: concise, observant, slightly philosophical, and materially connected to the pictured food. For an element-collage or still-life page with four or more distinct clusters, use one or two original short lines tied to separate clusters or to their shared material idea; keep total text sparse.

- Derive the thought from a real feature of the subject: ripening, seed, sharing, warmth, glass transparency, reflected light, flame, shelter, growth, layers, patience, or transformation.
- Use 5–15 words, one sentence, present tense, and plain evocative English. Favor a calm insight over a joke, slogan, command, or pun.
- Do not make health, medical, moral, or factual claims. Do not use famous quotations or wording that closely resembles them.
- Generate the fortune before rendering. Return one line as `Fortune: “…”`; if still-life mode uses two lines, return them as `Fortune 1` and `Fortune 2`.
- Render the illustration text-free, then add the exact approved fortune afterward in a small vintage typewriter-like serif face, charcoal or faded cobalt. Break it into 2–4 short fragments when this strengthens the visual path. Keep reading order obvious and never split a word. Vary alignment, rotation, and position subtly; do not place every fortune in one fixed corner. Never rely on an image model to spell the fortune correctly without verification.

Examples: apple — “What ripens slowly remembers the sun.”; coffee — “Some answers arrive only after the water warms.”; kiwi — “Small seeds can make a bright beginning.”; bread — “Time gives warmth a place to rise.”

## Quality Bar

- In food-photo mode, the food is immediately recognizable and visually dominant. In element collage mode, every named item appears as a distinct recognizable vignette; none should dominate by more than twice the scale of another.
- The image reads as a poetic scrapbook collage, not an inventory, near-copy, menu illustration, or decorative ingredient chart, while remaining identifiable as the original prepared dish.
- The source background is absent rather than merely blurred.
- A breakdown contains no more than three selected details and adds understanding instead of turning into a complete ingredient inventory; it does not replace the actual dish.
- Paper texture, translucent pigment, and imperfect wash edges are visible at normal viewing size.
- The paper reads clean cool-white rather than yellow parchment. At least 55% remains unpainted; no global ochre, sepia, or cream overlay shifts the entire page warm.
- Yellow or orange occurs only where the named subject, flame, or direct light calls for it; white, glass, ceramic, and empty paper remain neutral or lightly gray-blue.
- Major shapes show gently inaccurate geometry, partial or vanishing contours, paper gaps, and nonuniform pigment density. The result must look painted rather than filtered or digitally shaded.
- Repeated details vary visibly; no plate rim, seed ring, bird pair, lampshade motif, or vessel set is mechanically identical.
- The page has natural visual rhythm, asymmetry, and generous unoccupied paper. Food mode uses strong scale contrast; still-life mode may use several related-scale islands.
- Motion comes from spatial rhythm—placement, repeated form/color, overlap, and the natural direction of food shapes—not a forced connector line.
- In food-photo mode, one to three food-derived echoes and two to four restrained associative marks support the hero without competing with it. In element collage mode, avoid unprompted decorative marks.
- The page feels airy but inhabited: one hero plus a small, edited group of supporting elements, never a complete ingredient inventory.
- The palette is intentionally limited and the rendering retains naive irregularity rather than polished realism.
- The composition is chosen from the composition system and does not mechanically repeat a plate-and-caption arrangement.
- Element collage pages read as several airy mini-scenes separated by paper and type, not as one large central food illustration with leftover props.
- Each fortune is original, readable, 5–15 words, and clearly tied to its food, object cluster, or shared material idea rather than being a generic inspirational caption.
- Exclude illegible generated text, logos, UI fragments, frames, and accidental photorealistic debris.

## Still-life Test Adaptation

Use this mode when the user supplies non-food objects or a still-life photo to test the visual language. Treat the page as a **Watercolor Symbol Diary / 水彩符号手帐**, not as a still-life painting.

- Keep 3–6 object families and distribute 4–6 separate diary islands. Let objects float on paper; use at most one or two faint wash patches and no tabletop, horizon, cast shadow, or grounding halo.
- Give every object only the minimum marks needed for recognition. At least half of the objects should read like flat hand-painted icons rather than rendered products.
- Render clear glass as an irregular broken gray-blue outline, a short waterline or pale bottom pool, and visible stems. Omit realistic reflection, refraction, rim shine, wall thickness, and glass shading.
- Reduce plants to thin uneven stems and one to three flat translucent leaves or flowers per vessel. Suggest veins only when they identify the plant.
- Reduce lamps, sconces, and candlesticks to pale geometric silhouettes. Use one simple flame shape and a few rough, unequal motifs; never polish the hardware or model the light.
- Scatter 6–14 tiny faded-cobalt stars, asterisks, dots, or dashes between clusters. Make them handmade and individually different. They create pauses and rhythm, not a path or constellation line.
- Keep cluster scales related, approximately 0.7–1.6 times one another. No object needs to dominate the page.
- Use one short fortune, or two only when four or more distinct clusters need separate thoughts. Add exact text in the second pass and leave more paper than text.

## Element Collage Adaptation

Use this mode when a user gives a compact list such as “watermelon, tomato, shaved ice, wind chime” and expects a reference-like illustrated page rather than a unified photographed meal.

- Draw 4–6 separated mini-scenes. Give each named element a clear place; repeat only one named food as a simple variation when an additional vignette is needed.
- Keep groups loosely balanced across the page. The largest vignette may be at most twice the smallest; reserve roughly half the page as unpainted paper and text gaps.
- Make each scene feel like a childlike observation: a plate of slices, one loose fruit wedge, a small bowl of ice, a hanging chime, or a few tomatoes on a textile patch. Do not combine all food into a new recipe unless the user asks.
- Use no generic stars, dots, botanical sprigs, orbit lines, torn-paper pools, or background washes by default. Only add a quiet pattern or tiny motif if it belongs to a named object, such as basket weaving, a fabric stripe, or a wind-chime tag.
- Use two distinct 5–15 word fortunes for a page of five or more vignettes; place them in separate paper gaps, in small charcoal typewriter-like serif. The lines may share a season or material idea but must not duplicate one another.

## Example Direction

For a photo of a strawberry shortcake: make the plated slice recognizable but imperfect; let two strawberry states fall diagonally toward it, echo the seeds as tiny faded stars, place one cream-and-sponge cross-section on a pale torn-paper wash, and weave the fortune through the remaining paper in short typewriter fragments.
